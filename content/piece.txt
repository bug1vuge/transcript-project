On keeping voicemails

My grandmother left me eleven voicemails in the last year of her life. I didn't listen to most of them at the time. They were short, and they were always the same: it's only me, nothing important, call when you can.

After she died, I moved them to a folder on my laptop and gave each one a date. I play one when I can't sleep.

What surprises me is how little the words matter. It's the rest of it I keep returning to. The kettle behind her. The breath she takes before my name. The way she says goodbye twice, as if the first one might not have reached me.

A letter tells you what someone meant. A voice tells you how they were: that afternoon, standing in that kitchen, holding a phone that was slightly too heavy.

I used to think recordings were a way of keeping people. Now I think they keep something smaller, a single moment, exactly the size of a breath.

So this is mine. If you are listening, it is a Tuesday, a little after eleven, and the window is open. Nothing important. Call when you can.
